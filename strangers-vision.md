# Strangers

**A strategy consultancy with new ideas for strange times.**

We combine data, experience, and code to give executives the conviction to act on new ideas — while their competitors are still debating them.

---

## The Problem

Marketing organizations move too slowly. Too slow to adopt new technology, too slow to shift strategy, too slow to respond to market change. This isn't a talent problem or a creativity problem — it's a design problem.

Every major consultancy is built the same way: orient around client briefs, staff generalists against them, deliver recommendations, move on. The entire model is optimized to *capture revenue*, which means it's optimized to be agreeable. The result is an industry full of smart people generating predictable thinking on someone else's timeline.

Strangers is built upside down. We solve the speed problem in three ways — strong opinions, forward-deployed consultants, and strategy delivered as code — underpinned by a proprietary tech stack that compounds with every engagement.

---

## The Architecture

Three layers. All load-bearing.

### 1. Strong Opinions — A Living Ontology of New Ideas

Most consultancies start every engagement from zero. We start from conviction.

We build and maintain proprietary intelligence — named theses on where value is moving. Post Sale Experience Design. Business-Attention Hybrid Models. Category Creation. The 5%. Experience Density. AI as Product. These aren't blog posts. They're research-backed strategic positions, continuously updated, with real data and pattern recognition behind them.

**This is our inventory.** Where traditional firms sell time, we sell access to thinking that doesn't exist anywhere else. We show up with a point of view on what matters — so the conversation starts at a higher altitude from day one.

**Named growth models.** Not generic frameworks — proprietary theses about how brands actually grow. Each one is a bet we're willing to stake our reputation on. Each one is constantly fed with fresh evidence, tracked case studies, and emerging capability signals.

**Living case studies.** Not a reference library — a monitored portfolio of companies that prove the models. Tracked weekly. Every signal logged, every move connected to a thesis. When we walk into a meeting, we're not guessing what's happening in the market. We know, specifically, what changed this week and what it means for you.

**Intelligence that compounds.** Every engagement generates structured data: what we tried, what worked, which insight types cracked which problems, what language resonated with which kind of leadership team. Traditional firms lose this. We treat it as a compounding asset — pattern recognition that builds engagement by engagement and cannot be replicated by a firm that starts later.

**The next evolution: Shared R&D Projects.** Multi-client research initiatives where companies co-fund exploration of a thesis — learning from each other's data and context without sharing competitive information. This turns our intelligence layer into a platform, not just a product. It creates recurring revenue, deepens our proprietary knowledge with every engagement, and gives clients access to a quality of research no single retainer could justify.

The bet: within 2–3 years, the intelligence layer becomes the primary moat. Excellent consultants exist elsewhere. A compounding growth-model engine does not.

### 2. Forward-Deployed Consultants — Judgment at Altitude

We don't staff junior teams against senior promises. Every person we deploy has the judgment to sit across from a CEO and bridge the gap between a corporate agenda and a genuinely new idea.

**Forward-deployed means embedded, not advisory.** Our people operate inside the client's decision-making rhythm — not to learn the business (they already know business), but to translate our ontology into their specific context, in real time. No disappearing into a back room for 8 weeks. No big reveal. Continuous strategic output the client reacts to, redirects, and builds on. By the second meeting, we're already showing work that traditional firms take a month to produce.

**Senior because the stack amplifies judgment, and you can't amplify what isn't there.** The intelligence layer handles research, synthesis, pattern-matching, and pressure-testing against known frameworks. That means the consultant operates entirely in the subjective — the questions data can't answer. What cultural norms need to change? What barriers is the organization underestimating? What conventions can be broken? What is the room afraid to say? A junior strategist with this tooling produces faster mediocrity. A senior strategist, freed from intelligence work, produces the kind of thinking that normally requires 4–6 people and three times the timeline.

**Human because this is a relationship business and always will be.** The technology is invisible. The client experiences a person — unusually fast, unusually deep, unusually well-informed — who earns trust through demonstrated mastery, not credentials. The stack is the engine. The consultant is the product.

We stay small and senior by design. This is a margin choice and a quality choice simultaneously.

### 3. Strategy as Code — Proving, Not Recommending

Recommendations are a liability. The moment a strategy lives only in a deck, it becomes an opinion — debatable, deferrable, forgettable.

We deliver strategy as working prototypes, functional tools, and validated experiments. If we recommend a new pricing model, we build the calculator. If we identify a content strategy, we ship the first version. If we propose a new customer experience, we prototype it in code and test it with real users before the final presentation.

**This collapses the gap between recommendation and implementation from months to days.** It also changes the power dynamic: we're not asking clients to believe us, we're showing them evidence. The client doesn't have to imagine what the strategy means. They can see it, test it, and build on it. This is the difference between conviction and recommendation.

**Velocity is authority.** When you produce in days what normally takes weeks, you don't just save money — you change the client relationship. Speed becomes a credibility signal: "these people understand the problem deeply enough to move this quickly." And it creates time for what traditional timelines can't accommodate — more iterations, more pressure-testing, more creative exploration. Not cutting corners. Adding laps.

## Answers to the Hard Questions

**Scale:** Works at 3–5 consultants. Does it work at 20? Yes — but only if the tech stack scales with it. More FDCs means more engagements means more data flowing back into the intelligence layer. The system gets smarter with every consultant added, not more complex. The overhead is in building and maintaining the stack, not in coordination.

**Hiring: "Done it before. No interest in doing it the same way again."** The profile is a senior strategist who is restless at altitude — enough pattern recognition to have real judgment, but frustrated by how slowly their current firm lets them act on it. They've built things, not just presented things. They have taste, not just credentials. Key interview questions: *What have you built? What do you think needs to be made a bigger deal of? What's something you believed early that your last firm eventually caught up to?* The last one tests whether they lead or follow within an organization. If they can't answer it with specifics, they're not the person.

**Client education:** Don't sell differently. Sell like a traditional consulting firm — the credibility signals, the case studies, the senior presence in the room. Then deliver beyond expectations. The speed and depth become the story the client tells internally. You don't need to educate the market on a new model. You need to over-deliver so dramatically that the client becomes the evangelist.

**Data as product:** The intelligence layer exists to make the consulting better, not to become a separate revenue line. If it ever generates independent revenue, that's a bonus — but designing for it would distort the priorities. The data strategy serves the FDCs. The FDCs serve the clients. Keep the chain clean.

**IP and data sharing:** Make contribution a feature, not a concession. Clients who opt into the intelligence layer get ongoing access to the insights their category generates — even after the engagement ends. You're not extracting their data. You're inviting them into a network where their input makes the whole system smarter, and they benefit from that system indefinitely. The model is closer to a research consortium than a licensing agreement. Structure this in the engagement agreement from day one — not as a negotiation, but as an option they'd be foolish to decline.

**Offensive AI:** The escalation is organic, not designed top-down. As the intelligence layer gets sharper, FDCs naturally find themselves freed from research and synthesis work — and they start operating at a higher altitude. When they discover new needs at that altitude, those needs become the next build for the tech stack. The cycle is: stack frees the consultant → consultant operates higher → consultant identifies new opportunities → stack gets built to support those opportunities → consultant is freed again. The firm doesn't need a master plan for this. It needs FDCs who are paying attention and a tech team that builds fast.
